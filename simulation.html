    Assumptions and limits

    • Gravity enters only through the buoyant flow. Radiation, ignition, sample size and geometry are ignored.
    • Solid-fuel flame spread only. No droplets, gases or smoke.
    • All fitted outcome data is microgravity from BASS-II. The 0.165, 0.38 and 1 g panels are estimated, with no direct measurements. They rest on published NASA numbers for PMMA rods (a 1 g limit of 16% oxygen, a buoyant flow of about 20 cm/s). They are checked against one set of 1 g ground tests (SAFFIRE-II, concurrent flow at 20 cm/s and about 22% oxygen), not fitted to them.
    • Those rod numbers are applied to all four materials, which is an assumption.
    • BASS-II recorded no pressure. The fit assumes about 101.3 kPa.
    • Only four materials have flow-linked outcomes. Nomex, Ultem and wax appear in the data but without usable flow readings, so they are not offered.
    • The model does not carry over to materials it has not seen. See the validation summary below.
    • The thick PMMA slab fit is the weakest. Use that result with extra care.
    • How forced and buoyant flow combine differs between two NASA papers. The bands span both.
    • Known miss: NASA says downward flame spread over SIBAL does not happen in 1 g air. The model says it can. The result card warns when that case comes up.

    How the simulation works, and where it falls short

    Plain-language notes on what this tool does, where its numbers come from and what it cannot tell you. Sections open and close.

    What this is, and what it is not

    It estimates the chance that a flame on a solid fuel keeps burning, given the oxygen level, how fast air moves past the sample, the material and the strength of gravity. It is built from burn tests that astronauts ran on the International Space Station (the BASS-II experiment, PSI record 25), and from nothing else.

    It is a screening estimate from a simplified model. It is not a prediction of any real fire, it is not NASA-endorsed, and it should not be used for safety decisions. Its best use is to show where gravity might matter and which experiments would test that.

    How a result is calculated
    1. Gravity becomes an airflow. A flame heats the gas around it and the hot gas rises, which pulls fresh air in. In microgravity there is no rise, so no pull. The model turns gravity into an extra airflow, the buoyant flow, using U_b = U_1g × sqrt(g), where g is in units of Earth gravity. The square-root scaling and a 1 g value of about 20 cm/s (range 15 to 25) come from NASA papers on PMMA rods.
    2. The two flows combine. U_eff = (U_forced^n + U_b^n)^(1/n). One NASA paper derives n = 4. Another uses a plain sum, n = 1. The model's central choice is 4 and the bands sample everything from 1 to 4. The effective flow is then treated as if it were forced airflow in a microgravity test.
    3. A burn window in flow. Too little flow and the flame starves (it falls below a quench flow). Too much and it is blown off. The quench flow comes from fitting the BASS-II points and rises as oxygen falls. The blow-off flow is a straight line in oxygen taken from NASA numbers for rods: zero flow at about 13.4% oxygen, and 16% oxygen at the 1 g buoyant flow. The two limits meet at a minimum oxygen level, and below it nothing burns.
    4. Chance of burning. Two smooth steps, one at each edge of the window, multiplied together. Inside the window the chance is high. At either edge it falls through 50%.
    5. Limiting oxygen (LOC). The oxygen level at which the chance of burning is 50%, found by searching. The margin compares it with the same inputs at 1 g.
    6. Uncertainty. The model is refitted 200 times on resampled tests, with the NASA-anchored constants varied over the ranges those sources support, and extra spread added as gravity increases. The 5 to 95% bands are the spread of those 200 answers.

    Pressure acts only through oxygen partial pressure. Total pressure does not change the buoyant flow in this scaling.

    How to read the outputs
    • Chance of burning and the label. Burns at 65% or more, extinguishes at 35% or less, marginal in between. These cut-offs are assumed.
    • 5 to 95% range. The spread across the 200 refits. It does not include the model being the wrong shape.
    • Limiting oxygen here and at 1 g. Lower means the flame survives with less oxygen. The margin is the difference.
    • Hidden risk. Shown when the condition burns at the chosen gravity but would not at 1 g.
    • Maps. Shading is the chance of burning. The black line is the 50% oxygen level at each airflow, and the grey-orange band is its 5 to 95% range. Hatched areas are outside the oxygen and flow range of the data.
    • Dots on the 0 g map. Real BASS-II observations. Filled means the flame burned, open means it went out or blew off. A dark ring means the dot is on the wrong side of the line. A grey cross was read from the notes but left out of the fit, and hovering shows why.
    • Nearest real experiments. The closest observations by oxygen, flow, material, direction and thickness, each with the quote it was read from.

    The data behind it

    BASS-II has no usable airflow column in its table, and the dataset notes say the fan and air display values are not calibrated cm/s. So flow was read by hand from the crew notes. The air-display column is in time order, which made it possible to tell a flame that went out while flow was being reduced from one that did not. Each point has the quote it came from, and a test checks every quote against the raw table.

    For Earth gravity the model uses numbers published in NASA-hosted documents, mainly for PMMA rods, and applies them to all four materials. The only 1 g outcomes found are the SAFFIRE-II ground comparisons in PSI-99, at one condition (concurrent flow at 20 cm/s and about 22% oxygen) where SIBAL and PMMA burned the whole sample. They are used as a check, not to fit the model.

    Points left out of the fit, and why:

      The direction term (concurrent versus opposed) changed sign between two versions of the fit, from slightly negative to positive. Treat the direction setting as weakly supported.

      Limitations
      • Almost no outcome data above 0 g. The Moon, Mars and 1 g results are extrapolations. They rest on published NASA numbers for PMMA rods. The only 1 g outcomes are SAFFIRE-II ground tests at one condition, used as a check.
      • Gravity is reduced to one idea. Only the buoyant flow changes with gravity. How gravity affects radiation, ignition, soot and the shape of the flame is not modelled.
      • The blow-off line and the buoyant flow come from rods. The 16% limit and the 20 cm/s buoyant flow are NASA numbers for PMMA rods in stagnation flow. They are applied to film, slab and fabric, where they have not been checked. The 1 g and Moon answers depend on them heavily.
      • NASA papers disagree on how flows combine. One derives a fourth-power rule, another uses a plain sum. The bands cover both, but not a third rule.
      • One 1 g check fails. NASA reports that downward (opposed-flow) flame spread over SIBAL fabric is not possible in normal air. The model gives a high chance of burning. This is a direct sign that gravity acting only through buoyant flow is too simple, at least for thin fabric. The model does match the SAFFIRE-II 1 g ground tests, but those are concurrent flow near 22% oxygen, where nearly everything burns.
      • The 1 g limit is built in, not tested. The 16% figure was used to place the blow-off line, so the model matches it by construction. The checks that were not used are listed under How well it matches the data.
      • Small data.
      • Burns were long and changing. Many tests ran 10 to 30 minutes while the sample heated and shrank. The model treats each observation as a steady condition.
      • Samples differed in ways the model ignores. Width, one-sided versus two-sided burning and sample length vary between tests.
      • It does not carry over to new materials. Only PMMA film, PMMA slab, PMMA rod and SIBAL fabric are offered. Thick slabs fit worst.
      • Pressure was not recorded in BASS-II. The fit assumes about 101.3 kPa. One NASA paper also scales the mixed flow by pressure, which this model does not.
      • Solid-fuel flame spread only. Not smoke, droplets or gas flames. No spread rate is shown because the raw tables contain none for BASS-II.
      • Not checked in every browser. Tested in headless Chrome only.
      Glossary
      Microgravity
      The near-weightless condition on the ISS. Written 0 g here.
      Opposed flow
      Air moves against the direction the flame spreads.
      Concurrent flow
      Air moves in the same direction as the flame spreads.
      Quench flow
      The airflow below which the flame starves and goes out.
      Blow-off
      The flame is pushed off the fuel by too much airflow.
      Buoyant flow
      The airflow a flame induces by heating gas that then rises. Zero in microgravity.
      Effective flow
      Forced airflow and buoyant flow combined.
      LOC
      Limiting oxygen concentration. The lowest oxygen level that still supports burning in the given conditions.
      Brier score
      How far predicted chances are from what happened. Lower is better. Zero is perfect.
      Bootstrap
      Refitting the model on resampled data to see how much the answer moves.
      FM2
      Flammability of Materials on the Moon, a proposed lunar experiment. This page says nothing about its real hardware or plans.
      Check or reproduce it

      The model is in src/sim/model.js. npm run sim:calibrate refits it and rewrites the numbers on this page. npm run test:sim runs the unit and data checks. The points are in data/derived/bass2_flow_points.csv. The README has the full method and every validation number.
